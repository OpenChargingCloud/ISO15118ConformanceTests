# 2026-08-07 — eVDriveFlow over mutual TLS 1.3: the reason this counterparty was chosen, three years later

The capability eVDriveFlow was picked for, and the last of the four that sat behind
[the stdin wall](../2026-08-06-edf-stdin-wall/notes.md): **ISO 15118-20 over TLS 1.3 with a mutual
handshake**, verified by a second implementation instead of by ourselves.

| | |
|---|---|
| Counterparty | [`EDF-Lab/eVDriveFlow`](https://github.com/EDF-Lab/eVDriveFlow) @ **`60249c3`**, unpatched, with `SECURITY_PROTOCOL = 0x00` (their own switch) and their PKI regenerated by **their** `generateCertificates.sh` |
| Ours | conformance suite @ `aa55aa5`, `EVSimulatorApp` @ `d7a5ee4` |
| Direction | `←SECC` — their EV against our station, -20 DC, Dynamic, EIM |
| Outcome | **TLS 1.3 / `TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384`, mutual, secp521r1 on both sides — and 15 exchanges over it**, the same route as the plain-TCP run, ending at the same defect of theirs |

Artifacts: `tls.flow.md`, `tls.frames.log`, `our-station.tls.log`, `their-ev.tls.log`,
`shipped-pki-expired.txt`, and the two scripts (`regen-pki.sh`, `run-tls.sh`).

## What was negotiated

Read back from the live `SslStream` rather than assumed, and from their EV's own log on the other side:

```
ours   TLS: Tls13, TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384,
             client certificate DC=VEHICLE, C=FR, O=EDF, CN=VEHICLECert.
       presenting DC=CPO, C=FR, O=EDF, CN=SECCCert (+2 chain certificate(s))

theirs TLS Session established: TLS Version: TLSv1.3
       Cipher suite: TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384
```

Four things worth naming, because each has been an open bound in this project:

- **TLS 1.3**, which `docs/pki-model.md` pins -20 to, confirmed by a foreign stack rather than by our
  own two sides agreeing.
- **`TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384`** — one of the two suites the -20 profile prescribes, and the one our
  `TlsProfiles.Iso20CipherSuites` lists first. (Contrast the [tux-evse
  run](../2026-08-06-tux-tls/notes.md) the day before, where the peer's profile contained *neither* of
  the -2 suites and no handshake was possible at all.)
- **Mutual.** Their EV set `verify_mode = CERT_REQUIRED` against its own V2G root and presented
  `CN=VEHICLECert`; our station required a client certificate and read it back. Both directions
  authenticated, which is what -20 asks for and what nothing here had shown with a counterparty at -20
  before.
- **secp521r1 on both sides — the unremarkable thing that turned out to be rare.** Their whole PKI is
  P-521: `openssl ecparam -name secp521r1` on every line of their generator. That is simply what the
  standard says — ISO 15118-20 prescribes `ecdsa_secp521r1_sha512` or `ed448`, for the PKI and the key
  exchange alike (the app's [`docs/pki-model.md`](../../../libs/EVSimulatorApp/docs/pki-model.md) pins
  the same pair) — so it should be the boring half of this run.

  It is not, because **the other two -20 counterparties here both ship P-256**: Josev's PKI by choice,
  EVerest's `create_certs.sh -v iso-20` with a `TODO` beside it. Until this run every -20 TLS session
  this project had with anybody was carried by -2-grade key material, which is a property of their test
  PKIs rather than of their stacks. The pull towards P-256 is real and worth naming: **Schannel cannot
  use P-521 for TLS at all** (P-256 succeeds, P-521 fails "Authentication failed"), which is exactly why
  the app carries a second, managed TLS backend, and why its own Windows mutual-TLS tests use P-256. A
  test PKI that has to work on every platform ends up non-conformant almost by force.

  So the finding is not "we finally saw P-521". It is **"the first counterparty whose -20 PKI is the one
  -20 describes"** — and that says more about the state of -20 test material in the field than about us.

## The session over it

15 exchanges, every response code OK (`tls.flow.md`) — the same route as the plain-TCP run:

```
SupportedAppProtocol → SessionSetup → AuthorizationSetup → Authorization →
ServiceDiscovery → ServiceDetail → ServiceSelection → DC_ChargeParameterDiscovery →
ScheduleExchange → DC_CableCheck → DC_PreCharge ×3 → PowerDelivery → DC_ChargeLoop
```

…and it ends at the same place for the same reason: their charge-loop handler nulls its own target SOC
from our omitted optional `TargetSOC` and dies on `None * int`
([issue 2 of the report](../../reports/evdriveflow-headless-session.md)). TLS changed nothing about
that, which is itself worth knowing: the transport is not implicated.

## Finding — the certificates they ship expired in 2022

The first attempt failed in the handshake, and correctly:

```
ssl.SSLCertVerificationError: [SSL: CERTIFICATE_VERIFY_FAILED]
    certificate verify failed: certificate has expired (_ssl.c:1149)
```

Their repository's `shared/certificates/` holds material generated on 2022-08-07:

| | expired |
|---|---|
| `seccCert.pem` | **2022-10-06** — 60 days, which is exactly what ISO 15118 asks of a SECC leaf |
| `cpoSubCA2Cert.pem` | 2023-08-07 |
| `cpoSubCA1Cert.pem` | **2026-08-06** — the day before this run |
| `v2gRootCACert.pem` | 2047 |

So TLS with the shipped tree cannot work today and has not been able to since October 2022 — for
either role, since the SECC chain is the one a station serves. The fix is theirs and already in the
repository: `shared/certificates/generateCertificates.sh` regenerates everything (`regen-pki.sh` here
is a two-line wrapper around it). Worth a line in their README next to the TLS instructions, since the
error surfaces as a peer problem rather than as a stale checkout.

The 60-day SECC leaf is not a defect on their side — it is the standard's requirement, and it means
any checkout more than two months old has an unusable station certificate by design.

## How to reproduce

```bash
# their PKI, their generator (secp521r1 throughout, passphrase from their passphrase.txt)
bash shared/certificates/generateCertificates.sh

# our station serves *their* SECC chain
openssl pkcs12 -export -inkey privateKeys/secc.key -passin pass:123456789abcdefgh \
    -in certs/seccCertChain.pem -passout pass:interop -out secc.pfx

V2G_INTEROP_LISTEN=55000 V2G_INTEROP_PROTOCOL=20 V2G_INTEROP_MODE=dc V2G_INTEROP_DYNAMIC=1 \
V2G_INTEROP_NO_PNC=1 V2G_INTEROP_TLS_SERVER=$PWD/secc.pfx:interop \
V2G_INTEROP_TLS_REQUIRE_CLIENT=1 V2G_INTEROP_RECORD=/tmp/edf-tls \
  dotnet test -c Release ISO15118ConformanceTests.Simulation \
    --filter "FullyQualifiedName~EvDriveFlowInteropTests.TheirEvcc"

bash run-tls.sh   # their EV: SECURITY_PROTOCOL=0x00, the fresh PKI copied in,
                  # the SDP shim answering `tls`, and stdin held open (2026-08-06)
```

Two of those lines are the day's earlier findings paying for themselves: without the fifo the session
would stop at exchange 4, and without the regenerated PKI there would be no handshake to have.

## The same handshake on the managed backend — and that one is the point

The run above went through `SslStream`/OpenSSL, which happens to support P-521 on Linux. But the app
carries a **second TLS backend precisely for this profile** — BouncyCastle, TLS 1.3 only, the -20 suite
pair pinned in code, secp521r1 and Ed448 certificates — built because Schannel cannot do P-521 at all.
Until now it had never carried a session against anybody but ourselves.

Re-run with `V2G_TLS_BACKEND=BouncyCastle` and nothing else changed (`bc.flow.md`, `bc.frames.log`,
`our-station.bouncycastle.log`, `their-ev.bouncycastle.log`):

```
ours    TLS: their client certificate is DC=VEHICLE, C=FR, O=EDF, CN=VEHICLECert.
        TLS: BouncyCastle backend — TLS 1.3 with the ISO 15118-20 suite pair by construction

theirs  TLS Session established: TLS Version: TLSv1.3
        Cipher suite: TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384
```

15 exchanges again, service 6 again, ending at their charge-loop defect again. **So the -20-faithful
backend now has an external witness**: mutual TLS 1.3, both certificates secp521r1, the profile's own
suite — the one combination that had only ever existed in loopback, because no counterparty had the
material for it.

Two notes on what this does and does not assert:

- Our side **does not read back** the negotiated version and suite on this path — BouncyCastle's
  handshake result is not exposed through the `Stream` the transport returns, and the fixture says so
  rather than printing what it configured. The independent statement is their EV's log, which names
  both. What our side *can* say either way is whose certificate arrived, and it now does: the peer
  certificate is reported from the validation callback rather than off an `SslStream`, which is why the
  line is identical on both backends.
- The backend pins TLS 1.3 and refuses anything else, so "TLS 1.3 was negotiated" is not a discovery
  here — the discovery is that a foreign, standard-conformant peer **completes** that handshake, with
  P-521 credentials in both directions.

## What this closes, and what is left

- **Mutual TLS 1.3 at -20: done**, with a counterparty's own P-521 PKI.
- **Dynamic control mode over TLS**: same 15 exchanges as over plain TCP.
- Still open, and still theirs: the charge loop past `DC_ChargeLoopRes` (their `hasattr` defect), and a
  run whose verdict is specifically about **DC-BPT** rather than a by-product of the service their EV
  happens to select.
- ~~Untried on our side: the **BouncyCastle** backend.~~ **Done the same night** — see the section
  above. Both backends now carry this session against the same peer, which also makes them comparable:
  identical route, identical stopping point, the difference confined to the TLS layer.
